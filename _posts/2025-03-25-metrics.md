## Introducing Engineering Intelligence Tooling: A Journey

### Introduction

A couple of years ago, I was tasked with introducing **engineering intelligence tooling**—also known as **developer analytics platforms**, **engineering metrics dashboards**, or **productivity insights tools**—to better understand how developers spend their time. The goal was to leverage data to improve engineering workflows, identify inefficiencies, and provide better support to our engineering teams. However, I soon realized that this initiative was not just about selecting the right tool, but about earning the trust and buy-in from the engineers who would be directly impacted by it.

### The Need for Engineering Intelligence

The need for these tools arose from several challenges that engineering teams often face as they scale. Traditional one-on-one meetings often relied on anecdotal feedback and subjective impressions, making it difficult to get an objective view of how engineering time was being spent. With teams growing and projects becoming more complex, there was a need for a more structured approach to understanding productivity and areas of improvement.

By bringing data into our coaching conversations, we could better understand how developers allocate their time—whether they’re spending too much time on bug fixing, reviewing code, or working on new features. This insight helped engineering managers (EMs) provide more targeted coaching, reduce the risk of burnout, and ensure a more balanced workload across teams. Furthermore, these tools helped us make management less subjective and more data-driven, leading to fairer decision-making.

### Addressing Concerns and Building Trust

Despite the potential benefits, the introduction of engineering intelligence tooling was met with significant resistance, especially from junior engineers. Many feared that the tool would create an environment of unhealthy competition, gamification, and micromanagement. They were concerned that the data could be used to rank engineers or make employment decisions based on arbitrary metrics. These concerns were valid, and it was essential that we addressed them head-on.

#### Common Concerns Raised by Engineers

- **Gamification**: Engineers feared that knowing their work was being measured might encourage them to optimize for numbers rather than focusing on meaningful contributions.

  - **Solution**: We ensured engineers couldn’t see their own data, thus removing any temptation to game the system. Additionally, we emphasized that data would be used for coaching, not ranking.

- **Reduced Collaboration**: There was concern that measuring productivity could discourage engineers from spending time mentoring others or collaborating, which are essential for team success.

  - **Solution**: We made it clear that non-coding contributions were just as valuable as writing code. Coaching, mentoring, and reviewing were all recognized as critical activities.

- **Unhealthy Competition**: Engineers worried that the tool would create a competitive atmosphere that undermined teamwork and collaboration.

  - **Solution**: We ensured that engineers could not see each other’s data, and managers were trained to use the data for support, not for creating competition.

- **Fear of Misuse**: Perhaps the most emotional concern was that the data could be used to justify terminations or other punitive actions.
  - **Solution**: We assured engineers that the data would not be used to make employment decisions. It was a tool for improvement and coaching, not for penalizing individuals.

### Building Empathy and Maintaining Human Connection

While the tool provided valuable data, it was essential to ensure that empathy and human connection remained central throughout the process. The real value of engineering management lies in understanding the person behind the code. We reinforced this through several initiatives:

- **1:1 Conversations**: I personally spoke with engineers who had strong concerns, ensuring that their feedback was heard and addressed. This was crucial for building trust and reducing fears around the tool.
- **Manager Training**: We trained engineering managers not only on how to interpret the data but also on how to use it responsibly. They were encouraged to focus on support and coaching rather than evaluation.

- **Psychological Safety**: We continuously emphasized that the tool was meant to help engineers thrive, not to scrutinize them. Ensuring psychological safety was a top priority throughout the entire process.

### Data Sources and Integration

For the tool to provide meaningful insights, it needed to pull data from various sources across the engineering workflow. Some of the primary data sources included:

- **Version Control Systems** (GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket): Tracks commit activity, pull request cycles, and code review timelines.
- **Issue Tracking Systems** (Jira, Linear, Asana): Correlates engineering effort with project management tasks and backlog movement.
- **CI/CD Pipelines** (Jenkins, GitHub Actions, CircleCI): Provides insights into deployment frequency and build failures.
- **Code Review Platforms** (Phabricator, Crucible, GitHub PR Reviews): Captures participation in code reviews, review times, and PR cycles.
- **Incident Management Tools** (PagerDuty, Opsgenie, Sentry, Datadog): Highlights time spent on incident response and production firefighting.

By aggregating data from these sources, we were able to generate comprehensive insights into engineering activity, helping us better understand how teams were performing and where improvements could be made.

### Common Outliers and Why They're Not Necessarily Bad

In any dataset, there will always be outliers—data points that deviate significantly from the norm. In the context of engineering intelligence, outliers are not necessarily bad; in fact, they can provide valuable insights.

For example, an engineer who takes longer to review pull requests may be spending extra time mentoring or ensuring thorough code quality. Similarly, an engineer who produces fewer commits may be working on complex tasks or resolving bugs that don’t generate as many commits. These outliers may indicate unique, positive behaviors that contribute to the team’s success but don’t show up in typical productivity metrics.

Rather than penalizing these outliers, we viewed them as opportunities to zoom in and understand why certain patterns were emerging. By recognizing the context behind the data, we could provide more targeted support and ensure that valuable contributions were acknowledged, even if they didn’t fit the typical mold.

### DORA, SPACE, and Other Metrics

To assess engineering effectiveness, many of these tools rely on established metrics frameworks, such as **DORA** and **SPACE**, to provide a more holistic view of team performance.

#### DORA Metrics (DevOps Research and Assessment)

The DORA metrics are widely used to measure DevOps performance and effectiveness:

1. **Deployment Frequency**: How often the team releases new code.
2. **Lead Time for Changes**: How long it takes for code to go from commit to production.
3. **Change Failure Rate**: The percentage of changes that fail in production.
4. **Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR)**: How quickly the team can recover from production failures.

These metrics focus on continuous delivery and operational efficiency, helping teams understand how fast and reliably they can deliver value to customers.

#### SPACE Framework

The **SPACE** framework offers a broader view of developer experience:

1. **Satisfaction and Well-being**: How happy and motivated engineers are.
2. **Performance**: How well engineers are delivering on their tasks.
3. **Activity**: How much work is being done (e.g., commits, PR reviews).
4. **Collaboration**: The extent to which engineers are collaborating and sharing knowledge.
5. **Efficiency and Flow**: How streamlined workflows are and how much time is spent on value-added tasks.

By using these metrics, we could assess both the health of our engineering teams and the effectiveness of our processes, ensuring that engineers were not only productive but also engaged and well-supported.

### Conclusion

After two years of using engineering intelligence tooling, we’ve seen a significant positive impact on our teams. These tools have helped us bring data-driven decision-making to the forefront, allowing us to better support our engineers and improve our workflows. However, it’s important to remember that these tools are not a replacement for human touch. At the end of the day, they are just that—tools. The real value lies in how we use them to foster a culture of trust, collaboration, and continuous improvement. By maintaining a balance between data and empathy, we’ve created an environment where developers can thrive, knowing that their growth and well-being are our top priorities.
